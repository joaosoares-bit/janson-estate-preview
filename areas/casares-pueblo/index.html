Casares, the village Blas Infante was born in

Casares is a white village in Málaga province, built on a rock at four hundred and thirty-five metres with the ruins of a castle above it and a protected mountain behind. Its historic centre has been listed since 1978. The housing is terraced village houses on steep lanes, and most incoming buyers are here for a restoration.

Casares is the mountain half of a municipality that also owns a beach, and the two halves have almost nothing in common. The village stands on a rock at four hundred and thirty-five metres, twenty-two kilometres by road from Estepona, under the ruins of a castle that has sheltered the people below since at least the thirteenth century. Its historic centre has been a Bien de Interés Cultural since 1978, and the official description is precise about the reason: the street pattern and the shape of the houses are a response to the ravines the village is built across.

Almost everything for sale here is a village house, and a village house in Casares is a specific object. They are narrow, tall and terraced, sharing party walls on both sides, with rooms stacked over three or four levels because there was never any width to build across. Roofs are Arabic tile, walls are lime-rendered, and windows are small by modern habit. Many have a roof terrace with a view no amount of money buys on the coast. What almost none of them have is a garage, a garden or a lift.

Daily life is compact and vertical. The village works on foot because it cannot work any other way, and the bars and the shop are a few minutes from every front door. Behind it, Sierra Crestellina is a Paraje Natural and Zona de Especial Conservación rising to the cerro de las Chapas at nine hundred and forty-two metres, and it holds the largest griffon-vulture colony in Málaga province, which is to say the birds overhead are not incidental. In the Sierra de la Utrera, the sulphurous Baños de la Hedionda open for the summer, free of charge and by booking only.

The caveats are the ones people underestimate. Restoration inside a protected centre is slower and more prescriptive than elsewhere, materials and details are not entirely your choice, and getting a builder's lorry to the door may be impossible. Winter is genuinely cold at this altitude. And the market is small: the municipality recorded 2,731 residents in the village in 2017 against 3,173 down at Casares Costa, so more of Casares lives by the sea than on the rock. Paula's read is that this is a project purchase and not a turnkey one.

The village is a single architectural argument: lime-rendered white walls, Arabic tile roofs, narrow frontages, and buildings that step down the ravines instead of sitting on top of them. Houses are terraced and vertical, generally three or four levels, with a patio or a roof terrace in place of a garden. Because the centre is protected, façades, roof materials and openings are constrained, and a restoration is a conversation with the Ayuntamiento rather than a free hand. Newer building sits on the village edges and is modest in scale. There is no apartment market here in the coastal sense.

February is cold, quiet and beautiful, and that is the honest description. The village lives around a handful of bars and a shop, the school run goes down the hill, and anything larger: a supermarket, a hospital, a builders' merchant: is in Estepona, twenty-two kilometres away. Walking here is transport rather than exercise, and it is uphill in one direction. In summer the village fills with day visitors and the evenings run late. The sound is swifts, bells, and from the miradores nothing at all.

It suits buyers who actually want the restoration: people who have taken on an old house before, artists and writers, and couples who want a Spanish village rather than an international one and are content to drive to the coast. It does not suit anyone who needs a garage at the door, level access or a house that is already finished. Nor does it suit a family that wants a beach in ten minutes, or an owner planning to let short-term while never visiting: a house on a lane like this needs someone who knows it.

What moves the price here

Casares village sits at the foot of this region's ladder, below Casares Costa on its own municipality's shoreline and well below Cancelada and Estepona old town. The position is about product rather than quality: a village house has no plot, no garage and no sea, and it usually needs work. What it buys instead is a protected setting and a view no coastal address can manufacture.

Whether the house has been restored to a standard a surveyor will sign off; how many levels and how steep the stairs are; whether there is any outdoor space at all, terrace or patio; how close a car can get; and whether the roof terrace has the open view or looks at a neighbour's wall.

Questions about Casares

What is it like to restore a house in Casares?

Slower and more prescriptive than on the coast. The historic centre has been a Bien de Interés Cultural since 1978, so façades, roof materials and window openings are constrained rather than free, and the Ayuntamiento is a participant in the design rather than a rubber stamp. Add the practical problem of getting materials up a lane a lorry cannot enter. None of that makes restoration a bad idea; it makes it a project with a timetable, and the buyers who are happy here budget for both.

↗

Can you park at your own front door in Casares?

Usually not, and it is the most common surprise here. The village grew across ravines, and its lanes were laid out for people and animals rather than for cars; the official description of the town explains the street pattern precisely as an adaptation to that terrain. In practice you park where you can and walk, sometimes uphill, sometimes carrying shopping. Before committing to a house, do the walk from the nearest realistic parking space with something heavy in your hands.

↗

Why does Blas Infante matter so much to Casares?

Because he was born here, at number 51 Calle Carrera, and Andalucía calls him the Padre de la Patria Andaluza. The house is now a museum holding his birth certificate, material on his life and work, and the original coal-fired kitchen. For a buyer this is not trivia: it is why Casares receives a steady stream of Spanish visitors as well as foreign ones, which keeps bars open outside the summer and gives the village an identity independent of the coast.

↗

Is the village of Casares too cold in winter?

It is genuinely colder than the coast, and buyers who visit in May are often unprepared for it. The village stands at four hundred and thirty-five metres with a mountain behind it, and old lime-rendered houses with small windows hold that cold well. A properly restored house with insulation, decent glazing and a working chimney is comfortable; an unrestored one in January is not. Ask to see the house in winter, or at least ask the neighbours what they heat with.

↗

Do more people live in Casares village or on its coast?

On the coast, by a clear margin. The municipality recorded 7,157 residents in 2017: 3,173 at Casares Costa, 2,731 in the village and 1,253 at El Secadero. That matters to a buyer, because it explains why village services are modest and why this market is thin: fewer people, fewer houses, fewer sales in a year. It also explains why the village kept its character: it was never the part of Casares that grew.
