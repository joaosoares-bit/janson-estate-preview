Casares Costa: two kilometres of coast and the Torre de la Sal

Casares Costa is the coastal third of Casares municipality: two kilometres of shoreline wedged between Estepona and Manilva, with five small beaches divided by a sixteenth-century watchtower. Most of its housing is gated apartment and townhouse schemes built since the 1990s, and more of the municipality's residents live here than in the village itself.

Casares is a mountain municipality that happens to own a beach. Its coastal frontage runs to just two kilometres, wedged between Estepona to the east and Manilva to the west, and the whole of it is split in two by the Torre de la Sal, a sixteenth-century watchtower standing on the shore. East of the tower are Piedra Paloma, which marks the municipal boundary, and Playa de la Paloma, reached across the arroyo Jordana. West of it are Playa de la Sal, Playa Chica under its low cliffs, and Playa Ancha, widening towards the mouth of the río Manilva.

Because the frontage is short, almost everything here was built in one generation. The stock is dominated by gated apartment schemes and townhouse rows put up from the 1990s onwards: three or four storeys, communal pools, underground parking, a great deal of white render and clay tile. Detached villas exist but are the exception rather than the rule. What you rarely find is a genuinely old building, and what you never find is a great deal of choice: this is a small market, and in a small market the number of homes for sale in a given month is itself a fact worth knowing.

Golf does much of the work of holding a population here. Doña Julia lies inside the municipality with its southern edge metres from the shoreline, and Finca Cortesín, on the Casares road, has hosted the Solheim Cup and the Volvo World Match Play on a course by Cabell B. Robinson. Between them they keep people in a stretch that would otherwise be entirely seasonal. Everyday shopping means driving into Estepona or Manilva, and the municipal offices are up in the village, twenty-two kilometres from Estepona by road. There is no town centre down here.

The honest caveats are scale and administration. This is a thin strip with a river mouth at one end and a municipal boundary at the other, so a walk that starts pleasantly can end at a fence, and the arroyo Jordana genuinely has to be crossed to reach the eastern beaches. Everything municipal, licences, the padrón, the plusvalía, is handled by an ayuntamiento based in a mountain village rather than on the coast. Paula's read is that Casares Costa is bought by people who have looked at both neighbours and wanted something quieter than one of them.

Almost everything here is post-1990 and communal. The pattern is a gated scheme of three or four storeys arranged around a pool, in white render and clay tile, with wrought-iron balconies and an underground garage, and townhouse rows in the same language on the plots behind. A few detached villas sit on the higher ground with views towards Gibraltar. Nothing is historic: the only sixteenth-century structure on this shoreline is the Torre de la Sal itself. Renovation stock is largely a matter of thirty-year-old kitchens and bathrooms inside otherwise sound buildings.

In July the beach bars are busy and the schemes are full; by February a good many apartments are shuttered and the sand belongs to walkers. The people who stay are golfers and retired residents, and they eat in Estepona or Manilva as often as they eat here. There is no square, no market day, no church bell at the end of the street: all of that is up in the village. The sound is surf, and the A-7 behind the front row. For anything beyond bread and a coffee, a car is not optional.

It suits buyers who want a beach address without a resort around it: retired couples, golfers who play Doña Julia or Finca Cortesín, and second-home owners who value quiet over choice. It does not suit anyone who needs a town within walking distance, a family looking for schools and shops on foot, or an investor who wants a deep, liquid market: two kilometres of coastline produce very few sales in a year, and a thin market takes time to sell into as well as to buy into.

What moves the price here

Casares Costa sits below Cancelada and below Estepona old town, and above Casares village itself. It is the entry end of this coastline, and it is entry for structural reasons: no marina, no town, a short frontage and a small, slow market. What it offers that inland addresses cannot is proximity: the sand really is at the end of the street, and on this coast that always carries weight.

How far the scheme really is from the sand, and whether the outlook survives the building in front of it; the age and quality of the render on an exposed frontage; whether there is a lift and a garage; how occupied the scheme is through the winter; and which side of the municipal boundary the plot falls on.

Questions about Casares Costa

How much coastline does the municipality of Casares actually have?

Two kilometres, between the municipal boundaries of Estepona and Manilva. That is a very short frontage by the standards of this coast, and it explains almost everything about the market: a small number of schemes, a small number of sales, and no room for a town to grow around them. The tourist office divides those two kilometres into five named beaches, separated by the Torre de la Sal, a sixteenth-century watchtower standing on the shore.

↗

Is Casares Costa actually in Casares, or in Manilva?

In Casares, but the boundary sits closer than most buyers assume, and Doña Julia Golf is the usual source of confusion: it lies inside Casares while part of its perimeter borders Manilva. The municipality matters more than the postal address, because it decides your ayuntamiento, your IBI, the plusvalía municipal on a future sale and which town hall issues a building licence. Have the nota simple confirm it rather than a brochure.

↗

What is the difference between Playa Ancha and Playa de la Sal?

Position and width. Playa de la Sal lies immediately west of the Torre de la Sal; Playa Ancha is the widest of the five and runs on towards the mouth of the río Manilva, with Playa Chica between them under low cliffs. East of the tower, Piedra Paloma and Playa de la Paloma are quieter, and reaching them means crossing the arroyo Jordana. If you intend to swim daily, walk all five before you choose which street to buy in.

↗

Do people live in Casares Costa all year round?

A significant number do, and the municipality's own figures show that this is not a marginal question: of 7,157 residents recorded in 2017, more lived at Casares Costa than in the village or at El Secadero. The coastal population is the largest of the three. What that does not mean is a lively winter high street: resident life here is domestic and golf-based, and the restaurants that stay open are spread along the coast road rather than gathered in one place.

↗

Is there anywhere to shop or eat in Casares Costa?

Not in the way a town provides. There is no square, no market day and no shopping street: what exists is a scattering of beach bars, a few restaurants along the coast road and the small commercial units built into the gated schemes. Anything larger means Estepona to the east or Manilva to the west, both a short drive. The municipal offices are not down here either: they are up in the village, twenty-two kilometres from Estepona by road.
