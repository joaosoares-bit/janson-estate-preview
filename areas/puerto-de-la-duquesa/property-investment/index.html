Puerto de la Duquesa · Property investment

Duquesa: a basin that is finished, a hillside that is not

Puerto de la Duquesa carries a demand leg that beach addresses on this corridor do not: more than 600 owners keep a boat in the basin as their usual mooring and come back through the winter to work on it. The basin cannot grow. The hillside behind it can.

Manilva

Start with the fact that distinguishes this basin from a beach urbanisation. The operator reports more than 600 users who keep Puerto de la Duquesa as their usual mooring, and a boat is not a seasonal object: it needs lifting out, antifouling, an engine service and somebody to look at it in November. The port has the yard, the crane and a seventy-tonne travel lift to make that possible on site. For an owner of a flat above those quays, that is a stream of people with a practical reason to be here in the months when the beach towns empty. It does not fill a calendar on its own, but it changes the shape of the year.

The second thing to underwrite is where competing supply comes from, and here the answer is not the water. The basin is a built basin: its 328 berths and its quayside ring were finished long ago, and nothing new can appear on the frontline you bought into. Behind it, the Manilva hillside has been under residential construction for decades and continues to be, so a generic two-bedroom flat at Duquesa is competing not with another quayside flat but with something newer and inland at a similar posture. What protects a marina apartment is the thing the hillside cannot copy: the water in front of it and the walk out of the door.

Demand shape and liquidity in this basin

The year has three unequal parts. Midsummer is intense and short, and it is the only part a spreadsheet ever models. Spring and autumn are carried by golf: the course above the port opened in 1986 to a Robert Trent Jones design and pulls play into the weeks either side of the beach season. Winter belongs to the port itself, with more than 600 owners mooring here habitually and a yard capable of lifting seventy tonnes, there is maintenance traffic in the months when a purely seasonal address has none.

Liquidity is the strongest argument for this address and the weakest for a fast turn. Entry sits in the corridor's lowest band, the units are small and the buyers are users, so a correctly priced flat with a clean community file finds someone inside a season. But the same accessibility means the market is broad rather than scarce, and inland Manilva keeps adding phases at a comparable posture. Underwrite a medium horizon, expect to sell to somebody who wants to live in it, and treat any berth that comes with the flat as a differentiator rather than as income.

How this runs, step by step

  1. Step 1

    Name the demand you are buying

    Write down, before you view anything, which of the three demand streams you intend to serve: the midsummer visitor, the boat owner using the port through the year, or a long residential tenant. They want different flats in different rows at different specifications. An apartment bought to half-serve all three ends up disappointing each of them and is the most common underwriting error made at this address.

  2. Step 2

    Underwrite the block, not the flat

    Before any income assumption, take three years of the community's accounts and minutes and read them for what has been deferred. On a basin, the standing candidates are the lift, the waterproofing above the garage and the façade. A block with a healthy reserve and recent works costs more monthly and less in total; a cheap block with an ageing file is a bill waiting for a date.

  3. Step 3

    Take the berth out of the model

    Specific to Manilva

    A mooring at Puerto de la Duquesa is a separate asset with its own paperwork, and the port caps vessels at twenty metres with an entrance depth of between 1.2 and 3.5 metres. Do not build a berth into a return calculation unless you hold documented rights to one. Where a berth genuinely does come with a flat, treat it as a differentiator at exit rather than as a line of income.

    ↗

  4. Step 4

    Test the exit against the hillside

    Specific to Manilva

    Look at what is being licensed and built inland in Manilva before you commit, because that is your competition at resale rather than another quayside flat. Residential construction has been the leading sector of this municipality's economy for decades, and each new hillside phase arrives with warranties, insulation and parking. Ask what your flat will still have that the newest phase will not, and buy for that answer.

    ↗

  5. Step 5

    Model the quiet months first

    Specific to Manilva

    August is the easy part of the year and it is not where an investment is won or lost here. Build the model from the shoulder instead: the golf course above the port, laid out in 1986 to a Robert Trent Jones design, carries spring and autumn play, and the boat owners generate winter traffic. If the numbers only work with a full summer, you have bought a seasonal asset and should say so out loud.

    ↗

  6. Step 6

    Write down your exit buyer

    Decide now who buys this flat from you and why. At Duquesa the answer is nearly always a user rather than another investor, which has one practical consequence: presentation, condition and the community file matter far more at exit than a letting history does. Keep the works up to date and the paperwork in one folder from the first year, and the exit looks after itself.

What drives return at Duquesa is the length of the earning year rather than the height of any single week: the port keeps people arriving outside the beach season, and the walk-everything layout means a small flat performs above its size. What caps it is supply behind the water. Manilva keeps building inland at a similar posture, so a flat with no distinguishing feature always has a newer alternative nearby, and it competes on price. Running costs matter more here than further up the ladder: the community charge, the IBI set by Manilva and management weigh heavily against an entry-band asset.

Questions about this in Puerto de la Duquesa

Does the port generate demand outside the summer?

Yes, though not the kind that fills a summer calendar. More than 600 owners use this basin as their habitual mooring, and boats need lifting, painting and servicing at times of year that have nothing to do with holidays. The port carries a yard, a crane and a seventy-tonne travel lift for exactly that. The traffic it produces is steady rather than heavy, and it is the main reason winter here feels occupied rather than shuttered.

↗

Will more building inland hurt my resale here?

It sets the ceiling rather than removes the buyer. Nothing further can be built on the quayside ring, so the water in front of a frontline flat is genuinely finite. Inland Manilva, though, has had residential construction as its leading economic sector for decades and keeps producing newer stock at a comparable posture. The consequence is that an ordinary flat competes on price with something newer, while a flat with a real advantage, the basin, the walk, a documented berth, does not.

↗

Which airport will my guests actually fly into?

Often Gibraltar rather than Málaga, and that matters more than it sounds. The port puts Gibraltar's airport at about thirty kilometres and Málaga's at about ninety-five, so the shorter transfer belongs to a much smaller airport with a narrower route map. In practice a guest from the United Kingdom may find Gibraltar convenient while a Dutch or Scandinavian guest lands at Málaga and drives an hour and a quarter. Budget the transfer honestly when you set expectations.
